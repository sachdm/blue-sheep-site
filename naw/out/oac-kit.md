## Decisions

- **Tax credits: not on this film.** OAC makes projects that seek tax credits ineligible. On this budget the credits would likely come to less than the grant after grants reduce the labour base, and both need a Canadian distributor or broadcaster deal (a festival run does not count). Use credits on Together, Alone and Late August instead.
- **Deadline.** Oct 8, 2026 only if NOMAD gives up the slot (one application per artist per deadline). Otherwise the March 2027 deadline, results ~early Aug, shoot mid-September 2027 in real early-autumn light, which also fixes the script season problem. Confirm with TAC that a September 2027 shoot fits their grant period.

## Draft answers

Brackets are facts only you can fill in.

### Project summary (one or two sentences)

Not Another Word is a 12-minute narrative short about a celebrated writer who tells an interviewer the world would be better off without him, and the people across from him agree, because they have come to arrange exactly that. It will be shot in Toronto in one apartment, one pub and one street, over five and a half days.

### Project description (what you will make)

Miles Goodman is a famous writer who has become his own myth. The film follows one day of his life, from a 3:00 PM alarm to the next morning, and that day is a ritual: the first drink, the radio, the first cigarette, the view from the patio, dinner alone at the same pub. The first half of the film plays the ritual intact. The second half takes it apart one piece at a time.

Small signs gather that Miles notices and files away. A white van that never moves. His booth taken at the pub. A waitress who is kind to him and not allowed to say why. That night a three-person crew arrives to interview him. Asked what comes next, he says the myth would be better served by his death. They agree, and they have already printed tomorrow's paper.

The last scene returns every opening image to its place: the glass washed, the painting straight, the radio reading the news. The only thing missing is the man. The film is a closed loop, and the horror is in how orderly it is.

### Artistic merit (vision, approach, why now)

The question behind the film is what happens when a system takes a person entirely at their word. Miles says, half joking, that dying would be good for his legacy. An institution has been listening and agrees. It is a film about the market for a finished life, where an obituary can sell more books than the work did.

My approach is restraint. The film stays in one apartment for most of its running time, and almost every effect comes from performance, blocking and sound. The radio news that opens the film returns in its last seconds with his name in it. Props and the room itself carry the plot. The glass Shari sets out in the first shot is washed and waiting for nobody in the last. The organisation is never explained. The audience assembles it from what Miles misses.

The piece extends my work as a director into sustained two-hander performance and into formal structure: a story built as a ritual that repeats and degrades. It is wholly original, and the copyright is mine [confirm: director-held, with the co-writer's agreement on file].

### Impact (on your practice, collaborators and audiences)

For my practice, this is the first film where the whole design rests on one lead performance across twelve pages. Two audition days and a dedicated performance day are built into the plan so I can work with the actor in depth rather than for coverage. What I learn here feeds directly into my first feature, Late August, planned for 2027–2028.

For collaborators, the budget pays a Toronto cast and crew under ACTRA's Independent Production Agreement and at fair crew rates, and it names every in-kind contribution at market value. A grant from OAC lets the film pay deferred artist fees in full.

For audiences, the film is aimed at Canadian and international short-film festivals, starting with TIFF Short Cuts, then the Canadian circuit. Every screening copy will be captioned. The subject, the way a public figure's death can be worth more than their life, speaks to anyone who has watched a legacy being sold.

## What to tighten first

1. **Do the copy pass and number a v1.4 shooting draft.** Fix "Hours empty now", "peddling" and "vespa", and pick Chivas or Chartreuse. A panel reads the script first, and small errors cost more than they should.
2. **Settle the season in the script.** If you take Route B, the script is already right. If you shoot in winter or spring, rewrite the patio, the ride and the dawn for the actual season before you submit.
3. **Get the four secured items on paper.** A one-paragraph letter each from the DP, the AD and whoever owns the camera package, plus the co-writer's rights agreement. Secured with letters is worth far more than secured in an email.
4. **Pay artists in full on the page.** Keep deferrals small and say they are paid first. Councils fund fair pay, and a budget that leans on unpaid artists reads as a viability risk.
5. **Choose the past-work clip today.** This decision has been deferred since August. Pick one sustained scene that shows direction of actors, not a montage.
6. **Name the DP and AD with one line each.** Two short bios with their best credits. A panel trusts a first-time budget more when the heads of department have shot before.
7. **Make the form page.** One page with the clock-face dial and the six bookends from the continuity deck. It shows design thinking no other application in the pile will have.
8. **Keep the Hunter S. Thompson question closed.** The real-person attribution is already gone from the short. Do not mention the inspiration anywhere in the application, so no juror raises a rights question.
9. **Check the collaborator rule.** Anyone named on NOMAD's application cannot be named on this one at the same deadline. That matters only on Route A, and Cairns Nolan co-wrote both slates.
10. **Write every answer outside the portal.** Draft in a document, get two cold reads, then paste into Nova in one sitting a few days early.
